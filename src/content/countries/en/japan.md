## The kimono and its relatives {#kimono}

In Japanese, traditional clothing is called *wafuku* (Japanese clothes), as opposed to *yōfuku* (Western clothes). The central garment is the **kimono**, which literally means "thing to wear". It is cut from a long, narrow bolt of cloth into straight panels and sewn into a T-shaped robe. The modern kimono developed from the *kosode*, a robe with small sleeve openings that became the main outer garment by the Edo period (1603–1868).

A kimono is always worn with an **obi**, a wide sash tied at the back, and usually over an under-robe (*nagajuban*). Split-toe socks called *tabi* and sandals such as *zōri* or wooden *geta* complete the outfit. For formal occasions, men wear a kimono with *hakama* (wide pleated trousers) and a *haori* jacket. ## Wedding attire {#wedding}

At a traditional Shinto wedding, the bride usually wears a **shiromuku**, an all-white kimono ensemble. White stands for purity and a new beginning. She may cover her hair with a white hood (*wataboshi*) or a headdress called a *tsunokakushi*. Many brides later change into an *iro-uchikake*, a richly coloured and embroidered over-robe, for the reception.

The groom traditionally wears a black *montsuki* kimono marked with his family crest, together with a *haori* jacket and striped *hakama*. Many Japanese couples today hold a Western-style or mixed ceremony, but the shiromuku remains a popular choice for the shrine ceremony itself.

## Festivals and celebrations {#festivals}

Several yearly events are closely tied to traditional dress:

- **Coming of Age Day** (*Seijin no Hi*), on the second Monday of January, celebrates young adults. Many young women wear a **furisode**, a kimono with long, swinging sleeves.
- **Shichi-Go-San** ("seven-five-three"), around 15 November, marks the growth of children aged three, five and seven. Children are often dressed in kimono for a visit to a shrine.
- **Summer festivals** and fireworks displays are the season of the **yukata**, a light, unlined cotton kimono that is easy to put on and comfortable in the heat.
- **New Year** shrine visits are another popular occasion for wearing kimono.

## Kimono etiquette {#etiquette}

The most important rule is that the left side of the kimono is wrapped **over the right**, for both women and men. Wrapping right over left is reserved for dressing the deceased at funerals.

Kimono also follow levels of formality, set by the type of kimono, where its patterns are placed and how many family crests (*kamon*) it carries, from one to five. A black kimono with five crests and patterns only below the waist (*kuro-tomesode*) is the most formal kimono for married women, while the furisode is the most formal for unmarried women. A kimono covered in a small repeating pattern (*komon*) is suitable for casual outings.

## Traditional clothing today {#today}

Kimono are expensive and take practice to put on, so many people rent them for special days or ask a professional dresser (*kitsuke* specialist) for help. Kimono rental shops are common in historic districts such as those in Kyoto. The yukata is still widely worn every summer, and many traditional inns (*ryokan*) provide one to guests.
