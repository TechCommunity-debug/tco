## Andean highland dress {#highland-dress}

In the highlands, many Quechua and Aymara women wear a **pollera**, a full, gathered skirt that is often layered several times. Over the shoulders they wear a **lliclla**, a rectangular hand-woven shawl, usually fastened at the front with a pin (*tupu*). A fitted jacket, often embroidered, and a woven belt (*chumpi*) complete the outfit in many places.

Men's dress typically includes trousers and a jacket of homespun wool, a woven **poncho** and a **chullo**, a knitted hat with ear flaps. Both women and men often wear *ojotas*, simple sandals that today are frequently made from recycled tyre rubber. Read more about the garment worn across the Andes on our [poncho page](/garment/poncho/).

Hats are one of the clearest signs of where someone comes from. The flat, round **montera**, the bowler hat, felt hats and embroidered caps all appear in different areas, and their shape and decoration vary between communities.

## Weaving traditions {#weaving}

Andean weaving has a history of thousands of years. Before the Spanish conquest, Inca men wore the *unku*, a knee-length tunic sewn at the sides, and women wore a wrapped dress (*aqsu*) held with tupu pins. Much of today's highland dress combines these Andean roots with elements introduced during the colonial period, such as sheep's wool and certain cuts of skirt and jacket.

Weaving is still practised in many communities, especially in the Cusco region and around Lake Titicaca. Women often spin with a drop spindle while walking or talking, and many weave on a backstrap loom. Natural dyes remain in use, among them red from the cochineal insect. On **Taquile Island** in Lake Titicaca, textiles are made by both women and men, and knitting is traditionally a men's task. "Taquile and its textile art" is inscribed on UNESCO's list of Intangible Cultural Heritage.

## Regional variety {#regional}

- **The coast**: here dress reflects Spanish, African and Indigenous influences. The *marinera*, often called Peru's national dance, is performed in couples with a handkerchief. In the northern style, women usually dance barefoot in full skirts and lace or embroidered blouses, while men wear a straw hat and a light poncho or jacket.
- **The Amazon**: the Amazon region is home to many Indigenous peoples, each with their own clothing traditions. The Asháninka, for example, are known for the *cushma*, a long cotton tunic, and the Shipibo-Konibo for the geometric designs called *kené* that appear on their textiles.

## Wedding attire {#wedding}

Wedding customs in Peru vary widely between cities and rural areas, and between regions and communities. In many highland villages, the couple and their guests wear their best festive clothing, often newly woven or embroidered for the occasion. A celebration can last more than one day and include music and dancing.

In cities, many couples choose a white wedding dress and a suit, and some add Andean textiles or accessories. Across the country, godparents (*padrinos*) often play an important role and may help with the costs of the wedding.

## Festivals and celebrations {#festivals}

- **Inti Raymi**, the Inca festival of the sun, is re-enacted every 24 June in Cusco, with performers in costumes inspired by Inca dress.
- The **Virgen de la Candelaria** festival in Puno, held in February, brings together thousands of dancers and musicians. Its costumes range from embroidered, masked outfits to local community dress. The festival is also inscribed by UNESCO as Intangible Cultural Heritage.
- Many towns hold patron saint festivals, where people wear festive dress and dance groups perform in matching costumes.

## Traditional clothing today {#today}

In many highland communities, especially among women, traditional dress is still worn every day, while others keep it for festivals and community events. In the cities most people wear Western clothing. Weaving cooperatives and centres, particularly in the Cusco region, work to keep techniques and local designs alive and sell textiles directly. If you want to photograph people in traditional dress, ask first.
