## The sari {#sari}

The **sari** (also spelled *saree*) is an unstitched length of cloth, often about 5–9 yards (roughly 4.5–8 metres) long, wrapped around the waist and draped over the shoulder. It is worn over a fitted blouse and an ankle-length petticoat, which holds the tucked-in pleats. The decorated end that falls over the shoulder is called the *pallu*.

There are many ways to drape a sari. The most widespread today is the *nivi* style, with pleats tucked in at the front and the pallu over the left shoulder. Other drapes belong to particular regions, such as the nine-yard *nauvari* of Maharashtra. Read more on our [sari page](/garment/sari/).

## Other traditional garments {#garments}

- **Salwar kameez**: a long tunic (*kameez*) worn with loose trousers (*salwar*) and often a long scarf (*dupatta*). It is associated especially with Punjab and northern India but is now worn across the country.
- **Lehenga choli**: a long, full skirt (*lehenga*) with a fitted blouse (*choli*) and a dupatta. It is common in Rajasthan and Gujarat and popular for weddings.
- **Dhoti**: an unstitched cloth wrapped around the waist and legs, worn by men. In the south, men often wear related waist wraps such as the *veshti* in Tamil Nadu and the *mundu* in Kerala.
- **Kurta**: a long, loose shirt or tunic, worn by men and women with trousers such as the loose *pyjama* or the close-fitting *churidar*.
- **Sherwani**: a long, fitted coat buttoned down the front, worn by men for formal occasions and weddings, especially in northern India.

## Regional variety {#regional}

Handloom weaving gives many regions their own textiles. Well-known examples include:

- **Banarasi** saris from Varanasi in Uttar Pradesh, woven in silk with brocade patterns in gold or silver thread (*zari*).
- **Kanchipuram** silk saris from Tamil Nadu, known for heavy silk and wide borders in contrasting colours.
- **Patola** from Gujarat, made with the double ikat technique, in which both warp and weft threads are dyed before weaving.
- **Kasavu**, the off-white cotton cloth with a gold border worn in Kerala, as a sari or as the two-piece *mundum neriyathum*.
- **Mekhela chador** from Assam, a two-piece outfit of a wrapped skirt (*mekhela*) and a draped upper cloth (*chador*), often woven from local silk.

Dress also differs between religious and Adivasi (Indigenous) communities within the same region, so local custom matters more than any national rule.

## Wedding attire {#wedding}

Wedding clothing varies widely by region, community and family. In many parts of northern India, brides wear a red or other deep-coloured lehenga or sari, often heavily embroidered, and red is widely associated with marriage and good fortune. Grooms there often wear a sherwani with a turban.

In South India many brides wear a Kanchipuram or other silk sari, and in Kerala many Hindu brides wear a cream-and-gold kasavu sari. Southern grooms may wear a veshti or mundu. Many Christian brides in India wear a white sari or a white gown, and Muslim brides in some regions wear a lehenga or the wide-legged *sharara* or *gharara*. Before many weddings, the bride's hands and feet are decorated with henna (*mehndi*).

## Festivals and celebrations {#festivals}

New or special clothes are part of many celebrations:

- **Diwali**: many families buy and wear new clothes, such as saris, kurtas or lehengas.
- **Navratri**: in Gujarat, women dancing the *garba* often wear the *chaniya choli*, an embroidered skirt and blouse that may be decorated with small mirrors. Men may wear the *kediyu*, a short, flared top.
- **Durga Puja**: in West Bengal, many women wear a white sari with a red border, especially on the last day of the festival.
- **Onam**: in Kerala, the kasavu sari and mundu are widely worn.
- **Eid**: families often dress in new clothes, such as kurtas, salwar kameez or sherwanis.

## Traditional clothing today {#today}

In cities, many people wear Western clothes to work or school, but the sari and the salwar kameez are still everyday wear for many women, and kurtas are popular with men and women alike. India marks National Handloom Day on 7 August to promote handwoven textiles, and many designers reinterpret regional weaves and silhouettes for modern wear.
