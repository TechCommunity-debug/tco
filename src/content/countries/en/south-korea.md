## The hanbok {#hanbok}

The Korean word *hanbok* simply means "Korean clothing". In North Korea the same dress is usually called *joseon-ot*. The basic outfit has two parts: a short jacket called a **jeogori** and, for women, a full wrap skirt called a **chima**, or for men, roomy trousers called **baji**. The jacket is closed with a pair of long ribbons, the *goreum*, tied at the chest.

For going out or for formal occasions, a long overcoat called a *durumagi* is worn on top. The shapes most people recognise today go back largely to the Joseon dynasty (1392–1897), when the women's jeogori gradually became shorter and the chima fuller. Read more about its parts and history on our [hanbok page](/garment/hanbok/).

## Colours, rank and accessories {#accessories}

In the Joseon period, clothing showed a person's place in society. The royal family, court officials and the aristocratic *yangban* class wore silk in colours and patterns set by rank, while ordinary people mostly wore undyed or white cotton and hemp. White clothing was so common that Koreans were sometimes described as the "white-clad people".

Traditional colour symbolism draws on *obangsaek*, the five cardinal colours: blue, red, yellow, white and black. They appear in the **saekdong** jacket, whose sleeves are made of multicoloured stripes and which is often worn by children. Common accessories include:

- **Norigae**: a decorative pendant with tassels, hung from the jacket ties or the skirt band.
- **Binyeo**: a long hairpin that held a married woman's hair in a low bun.
- **Gat**: a wide-brimmed black hat made of horsehair and bamboo, worn by adult men in the Joseon period.

## Wedding attire {#wedding}

At a traditional Korean wedding (*jeontong honrye*), the bride wore a **hwarot** or a **wonsam**, ceremonial robes that were originally worn by women of the royal court. Brides of all classes were allowed to wear them on their wedding day. The hwarot is richly embroidered with flowers, birds and other motifs that stand for long life and happiness. The bride also wore a small crown (*jokduri*) or an ornate headdress, and red dots were painted on her cheeks and forehead. The groom wore the robe, belt and hat of a court official, known as *samo gwandae*.

Today most couples hold a Western-style ceremony, then change into hanbok for the **pyebaek**, a smaller ceremony that traditionally honoured the groom's family and now often includes both families. The couple bow deeply to their parents, who toss jujubes and chestnuts that the couple try to catch in the bride's skirt or a cloth, a wish for many children. The mothers of the couple often wear hanbok too, traditionally with the groom's mother in cool shades such as blue and the bride's mother in warm shades such as pink.

## Festivals and celebrations {#festivals}

Hanbok is most often seen on the big family holidays and at milestones in a child's life:

- **Seollal**, the Lunar New Year, usually falls in January or February. New clothes for the holiday are called *seolbim*, which traditionally meant a new hanbok, and many families still dress their children in one. Children perform a deep New Year's bow (*sebae*) to their elders.
- **Chuseok**, the harvest festival on the 15th day of the eighth lunar month, brings families together for ancestral rites and shared meals. Some families wear hanbok for the day.
- A baby's **first birthday** (*dol*) is a major celebration. The child usually wears a colourful hanbok, often with saekdong sleeves and a traditional cap, and takes part in *doljabi*: choosing one object from a table, which is playfully said to hint at their future.

## Traditional clothing today {#today}

South Koreans wear Western clothes day to day. From around the 1990s, designers also began making **modern hanbok**, sometimes called *saenghwal hanbok* ("everyday hanbok"), with simpler cuts, shorter skirts and easy-care fabrics suited to daily life.

Hanbok has also become part of visiting historic places. Rental shops near Gyeongbokgung and other palaces in Seoul rent hanbok by the hour, and visitors wearing hanbok have been able to enter the royal palaces free of charge. Many rental outfits use bright colours, shiny fabrics or modern details that differ from historical dress.
