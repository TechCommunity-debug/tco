## Many peoples, many styles {#regional}

China officially recognises 56 ethnic groups. The largest, the Han, make up the great majority of the population, and many of the others have their own distinct dress. In some rural areas it is still worn every day, elsewhere mainly for festivals and family events. A few examples:

- **Tibetans** wear the *chuba*, a long, wide-sleeved robe belted at the waist, often lined with sheepskin in the highlands.
- **Mongols** wear the *deel*, a long robe fastened at the side and suited to riding and a cold climate.
- **Miao** women are known for detailed embroidery and, in some communities, large silver headdresses and jewellery worn at festivals.
- **Uyghur** men and women often wear the *doppa*, an embroidered cap.

Dress also differs from region to region within the same group, so these are only broad outlines.

## Hanfu {#hanfu}

*Hanfu* ("Han clothing") is a modern umbrella term for the dress of the Han Chinese before the Qing dynasty. It covers many styles that changed over more than two thousand years, but most share a few features: a crossed collar with the left panel wrapped over the right, wide sleeves and a sash at the waist.

Since the early 2000s, a revival movement has brought hanfu back into public life. Young people wear reconstructed or modern versions for photos, festivals, graduation ceremonies and visits to historic sites, and many shops now make them.

## Qing robes and the qipao {#qipao}

From 1644 to 1912 China was ruled by the Manchu Qing dynasty, which brought its own clothing customs. Officials wore long robes with narrow sleeves and cuffs shaped like horse hooves, and embroidered dragon robes were worn at court. Women's informal jackets, like the 19th-century example in the photo above, were often made of silk and decorated with embroidery on the body and wide bands at the sleeves.

In the 1920s and 1930s, above all in Shanghai, the **qipao** developed into a close-fitting dress with a high collar, drawing on older long robes and on Western tailoring. In Hong Kong and in English it is often called the cheongsam. Read more on our [qipao page](/garment/qipao/).

## Wedding attire {#wedding}

Red is the traditional colour of weddings in China, standing for joy and good fortune. In the past a bride often wore red and covered her head with a red veil, which the groom lifted after the ceremony. In late imperial times, brides from well-off families might wear a *phoenix crown* (*fengguan*) and an embroidered cape (*xiapei*).

Today many couples in southern China, Hong Kong and overseas Chinese communities choose a **qun kwa** (Mandarin *qungua*) for the bride: a two-piece jacket and skirt in red, embroidered with dragons and phoenixes in gold and silver thread. A similar embroidered outfit is often called **xiuhe**. Many brides wear a white Western gown for part of the day and change into a qun kwa, a red qipao or a hanfu-style dress for the tea ceremony, in which the couple serve tea to their elders, or for the banquet. Grooms often wear a suit, a Chinese-style jacket or a long gown.

## Festivals and celebrations {#festivals}

- **Lunar New Year** (Spring Festival), in January or February, is the most important holiday. Many people buy new clothes for it, and red clothing and accessories are popular for good luck. Some people wear red during their own zodiac year (*benmingnian*), which is traditionally considered a year of bad luck.
- Traditional festivals such as the **Lantern Festival** and **Mid-Autumn Festival** have become popular days for hanfu enthusiasts to dress up.
- Many ethnic groups wear their finest dress for their own festivals and New Year celebrations.

## Traditional clothing today {#today}

Most people in China wear Western-style clothing day to day. Traditional elements still appear in many places. The **Tang suit** (*tangzhuang*), a jacket with a standing collar and knotted "frog" buttons, is often worn at New Year and family events. Despite its name, it is based largely on the *magua*, a short jacket of the Qing period. Qipao-style uniforms are used in some hotels and restaurants, and the qipao remains a common choice for formal events in China and abroad.
