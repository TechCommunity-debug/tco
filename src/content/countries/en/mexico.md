## Indigenous dress: huipil and rebozo {#huipil}

Mexico's indigenous peoples, among them Maya, Zapotec, Mixtec, Nahua and Otomí communities, have their own ways of dressing, and styles often differ from one town to the next. The best-known women's garment is the **huipil** (from the Nahuatl *huipilli*), a loose tunic made from one or more rectangular panels of cloth, traditionally woven on a backstrap loom and sewn together with openings for the head and arms. Its colours, motifs and embroidery can show where the wearer comes from.

Other garments of pre-Hispanic origin include the *quechquemitl*, a short shoulder cape worn in parts of central and eastern Mexico, and the wrap-around skirt (*enredo*). The **rebozo**, a long rectangular shawl, is worn by women across Mexico: over the shoulders or head, or tied to carry a baby or goods.

## Sarape, charro suit and china poblana {#sarape-and-charro}

The **sarape** is a long woven blanket, traditionally of wool, that men wear over the shoulder or as a cloak. The most admired historic examples come from Saltillo in northern Mexico. Read more on our [sarape page](/garment/sarape/).

The **charro suit** (*traje de charro*) developed from the dress of horsemen on rural estates. It has a short fitted jacket, close-fitting trousers decorated with silver buttons along the outer seam, a bow tie and a wide-brimmed sombrero. It is worn in *charrería*, Mexico's equestrian tradition, which UNESCO inscribed as intangible cultural heritage in 2016, and it is also the stage dress of mariachi musicians.

The **china poblana**, associated with the city of Puebla, is a women's outfit with an embroidered white blouse, a full red-and-green skirt often decorated with sequins, and a rebozo. A popular story links it to Catarina de San Juan, a woman of Asian origin who lived in Puebla in the seventeenth century, but the outfit as it is known today developed later. It has since become a national symbol.

## Regional variety {#regional}

- **Isthmus of Tehuantepec (Oaxaca)**: Zapotec women wear the **Tehuana** dress, a short huipil, often of velvet, embroidered with large flowers, with a long skirt finished in a white ruffle and, for festivals, gold jewellery. The painter Frida Kahlo often wore it.
- **Yucatán**: Maya women wear the *hipil*, a white cotton dress embroidered with flowers around the neck and hem; its festive version is the *terno*. Many men wear the **guayabera**, a light shirt with pleats and pockets.
- **Chiapas**: the highland Maya communities are known for dress that differs from village to village, from embroidered blouses to woollen skirts.

## Wedding attire {#wedding}

Most Mexican couples today marry in a white wedding dress and a suit, but many customs shape the ceremony. At Catholic weddings, sponsors (*padrinos*) often place a **lazo**, a large loop of rosary beads, cord or flowers, around the shoulders of the couple as a sign of their union. In many ceremonies the groom also gives the bride thirteen coins (*arras*).

In Yucatán and other hot coastal areas, grooms and male guests often wear a white guayabera, and some brides choose a dress inspired by the hipil. Some grooms wear a charro suit, and in many indigenous communities brides wear their community's festive dress.

## Festivals and dance {#festivals}

- **Independence Day** (16 September, celebrated from the night before): many people wear china poblana and charro outfits or clothes in the national colours.
- **Guelaguetza** in Oaxaca City, held in July: groups from the regions of Oaxaca dance in their own traditional dress.
- **Folklórico dance** (*baile folklórico*): stage costumes based on regional dress, such as the wide ribboned skirts of Jalisco, worn with a charro suit for the *jarabe tapatío*. The Ballet Folklórico de México, founded by Amalia Hernández in 1952, made these costumes widely known.

## Traditional clothing today {#today}

In parts of Oaxaca, Chiapas, Yucatán, Guerrero and other states, many indigenous women still wear the huipil and other community dress every day. Elsewhere, traditional garments are kept for festivals, dance and family celebrations, and embroidered blouses and dresses are also popular in everyday fashion.

Indigenous communities and the Mexican government have criticised international fashion brands for copying indigenous designs without credit or permission, and a federal law passed in 2022 aims to protect the cultural heritage of indigenous and Afro-Mexican communities, including their textile designs.
